Foundation

Fear & Greed
Why Most Traders Fail

From ancient spice routes to modern crypto exchanges, one force has driven every market throughout human history: the psychology of the participants. Technical analysis is essentially a real-time map of that psychology — each candle capturing a moment of collective euphoria, panic, greed or despair.

Every price movement distills down to a single truth: most market participants trade with emotions rather than evidence. When prices rise, greed whispers that they'll rise forever. When they fall, fear screams to sell everything — usually at exactly the wrong moment.

📈 The Winning Trader's Curve
Makes systematic, data-driven decisions. Drawdowns exist, but the equity curve trends steadily upward because each trade follows a framework — not a feeling.
📉 The Emotional Trader's Curve
Makes decisions based on news sentiment, social pressure and gut reactions. Gains are given back quickly. Each cycle of hope followed by panic leaves the account smaller than before.

Greed — Signals of an Approaching Peak

1
Assess your recent performance. If you've been making outsized gains consistently and it all feels effortless, take note. That feeling is likely shared by a large portion of the market — and shared euphoria historically precedes sharp reversals.
2
Scan the headlines. Overwhelmingly bullish news flow, lofty price forecasts and celebrity endorsements are classic markers of peak market optimism. When mainstream media publishes "can't lose" narratives, caution is warranted.
3
Listen to the crowd. When friends, family or coworkers who've never shown interest in markets suddenly ask how to get in on crypto, that widespread FOMO often signals the crowd is arriving at the top — the last buyers before the inevitable shift.

Fear — Signals of an Approaching Bottom

1
Monitor news sentiment. Pervasive pessimism — collapsing headlines, bankruptcy rumours, universal bearishness — can actually signal that a price bottom is forming. Historic examples include the 2020 COVID crash and the 2022 FTX collapse: both followed by dramatic recoveries.
2
Check in with your own emotions. Are you overwhelmingly bearish for no clear chart-based reason? Are you getting caught up in the prevailing narrative? Peak fear often lives inside you before it's visible on a chart — and a bottom may be closer than you think.

The SignalStrike Mantra: Extreme fear precedes financial opportunity. Extreme greed precedes financial risk. Know which side of the crowd you want to be on.

⚡ Action Steps

Spotting a near-term top (without charts)

① Your account is up significantly and it feels easy
② Every news story is bullish
③ Friends and family are asking how to buy crypto
Spotting a near-term bottom (without charts)

① The news cycle is saturated with negativity
② You personally feel afraid to buy anything

Chart Reading

Candlestick Language
Every Candle Tells a Story

Recognising chart patterns is the foundation of predicting market direction. And the fundamental building block of every pattern is the candlestick — a single visual unit that captures the entire battle between buyers and sellers within any given timeframe.

▲
Green (Up) Candle — The close is above the open. Buyers won the session. The body represents the open-to-close range; the upper wick marks the session high; the lower wick marks the session low.
▼
Red (Down) Candle — The close is below the open. Sellers won. Same body/wick structure, but the top of the body is the open and the bottom is the close.

Candlestick Anatomy · SignalStrike.online

What Each Candle Shape Means

Large Body, Small Wicks
One side dominated decisively. Large green body = strong buyer conviction. Large red body = sellers in full control with minimal pushback.
Small Body, Long Wicks
Market indecision. Price moved significantly in both directions but ended near where it opened. Signals a tug-of-war with no clear victor yet.
Red Body, Long Lower Wick
Bears pushed hard lower but bulls stepped in and rejected the move. The long tail shows buying interest at lower levels — a potential reversal signal.

Timeframes That Matter

TimeframeBest Used ForWeight
MonthlyLong-term macro trend direction and major cycle identificationHighest
WeeklyMedium-term swing setups and key structural levelsHigh
DailyShort-term sentiment, entry timing and confirmationMedium
Key Rule: Higher timeframes always take priority over lower ones. A compelling daily pattern means little if it contradicts the monthly trend.

Direction

Trend Identification
Uptrends, Downtrends & Patterns

A trend is the general direction in which a price is travelling. Markets move in three directions: up (bullish), down (bearish), or sideways (consolidation). Understanding which trend you're in determines your entire strategy.

Uptrend

Higher Highs & Higher Lows

Each rally peak surpasses the previous one, and each pullback holds above the prior low. Bullish psychology is building — buyers are increasingly willing to step in at higher levels.

Downtrend

Lower Highs & Lower Lows

Each bounce fails below the prior peak, and each selloff breaks below the prior trough. Bearish conviction dominates — each rally is weaker and less sustained than the last.

Chart Patterns: Similar Looks, Opposite Outcomes

Every trend is foreshadowed by specific recurring formations. Two patterns can look deceptively similar but signal completely different futures — a critical distinction that separates profitable traders from the crowd.

Bullish Continuation

Bullish Rectangle

Price consolidates horizontally between a defined range high and range low. The sideways pause is a recharge rather than a reversal. When price breaks above range resistance with conviction, the prior uptrend typically resumes — often with momentum.

Bearish Reversal

Rising Wedge

Price drifts upward with narrowing range — technically making higher highs and higher lows, but losing energy. When it breaks below the lower channel support, the result is often a sharp, accelerated sell-off. The upward drift was a trap.

Trendlines & Channels

Trendlines are slanted lines connecting successive price points — a visual representation of the collective sentiment shift unfolding in real time. They serve three purposes in active trading:

① Trend Direction
An upward-sloping trendline confirms bullish momentum is intact. A downward slope confirms sellers are in control. This is your macro compass.
② Dynamic Support/Resistance
Trendlines act as moving support or resistance zones. Each successful test of the line without a break confirms the trend's strength and creates a buying or selling opportunity.
③ Breaks & Reversals
When price closes decisively through a major trendline, the trend has likely shifted. Wait for a retest of the broken line — it should now behave as the opposite (support becomes resistance, or vice versa).
Channels are simply two parallel trendlines bounding price action — one connecting highs, one connecting lows. They let you apply the same buy-low/sell-high range trading logic you'd use with horizontal support and resistance.

⚡ 4-Step Process for Trendlines

1
Connect candle bodies, not wicks. This reduces false signals from temporary spike highs and spike lows.
2
Downtrend lines: Look for at least 3 consecutive lower highs to connect. This zone acts as resistance on each retest.
3
Uptrend lines: Look for at least 3 consecutive higher lows. This zone acts as dynamic support each time price pulls back.
4
Confirming a break: Wait for a candle to close beyond the trendline on that timeframe, then watch for a retest. Resistance flipping to support (or vice versa) is high-conviction confirmation.

Range Trading

Support & Resistance
Profiting from Trading Ranges

Sideways consolidation — where price moves horizontally between defined boundaries — is the most predictable market condition. Neither buyers nor sellers have enough conviction to break out, creating repeatable trading opportunities between the range floor and ceiling.

Identifying Support & Resistance · SignalStrike.online

Why Ranges Are So Tradeable
The emotional extremes of greed (at resistance) and fear (at support) produce predictable, repeatable behaviour. The crowd consistently overreacts — and that overreaction is your edge.
Key rule for plotting ranges: Connect closing prices on candle bodies, not wick tips. Wicks are noise; closes reflect conviction. A cleaner range means more reliable trades.

False Breakouts & Breakdowns

Understanding herd mentality is essential here. When price briefly pierces resistance, latecomers pile in expecting a continued rally — only for price to reverse back inside the range. The same dynamic plays out below support. These false moves are shakeouts that flush weak hands before the real move occurs.

The strongest emotional reactions in any market occur at range extremes. The sharp, fast moves that follow those extremes are exactly the trading opportunities you want to be prepared for.

⚡ Action Steps: Trading Support & Resistance

Buying at Range Lows

1
Dollar-cost average into your position as price approaches the range support zone.
2
Consider placing limit orders well below the range low — "stink bids" — to take advantage of any panic-driven capitulation wick.

Selling at Range Highs

1
Exit 50% of your position at range high resistance.
2
Exit another 30% slightly above resistance — into any FOMO wick.
3
Exit the final 20% at the FOMO wick extremes. This "averaging out" improves your overall exit price versus selling everything at once.

Confirmation

Moving Averages
Filtering Noise, Confirming Trends

Moving averages smooth out the inherent volatility of price data into a single flowing line, making it dramatically easier to identify whether an asset is in an uptrend, downtrend, or range. The two main types each have a distinct character and use case.

Simple Moving Average (SMA)
Calculates the mean closing price over a set number of periods, giving equal weight to every session. A 10-day SMA averages the past 10 daily closes. Smooth and simple, but slower to respond to new price action.
Exponential Moving Average (EMA)
Similar to the SMA but weights recent prices more heavily, making it faster and more sensitive to current market conditions. For volatile assets like crypto, the EMA provides better risk management and earlier trend signals.

The SignalStrike Recommended Setup

Overloading charts with moving averages creates noise and contradictory signals. Use exactly two per timeframe:

TimeframeShort-Term MALong-Term MAWhat to Watch For
Daily 50 EMA 200 EMA Price above rising 50 EMA = short-term uptrend intact. Price above rising 200 EMA = macro trend intact.
Weekly 21 EMA 50 EMA Holding above rising 21 EMA confirms short-term bull structure. 50 EMA is the deeper macro support level to watch.

Markets have a fractal nature — the same patterns and MA dynamics repeat across timeframes. A price respecting the rising 21 EMA on the weekly chart mirrors the same dynamic as price finding support at the 50 EMA on the daily. This consistency is your edge.

When to act: If price breaks decisively below key moving averages on your primary timeframe, the trend has likely shifted. Moving averages give you an objective, real-time signal to adjust strategy before losses compound.

Glossary
Every Term, Defined

B
Bearish
A market outlook or sentiment that prices will decline. Associated with downtrends and selling pressure.
Bearish Reversal Pattern
A chart formation suggesting an existing uptrend is about to end and reverse downward. Example: Rising Wedge.
Bottom
The lowest price point within a move, often signalling a potential turn upward.
Bullish
A sentiment or outlook indicating prices are expected to rise. Associated with uptrends.
Bullish Continuation Pattern
A formation suggesting an uptrend will resume after a consolidation pause. Example: Bullish Rectangle.
C
Channels
Two parallel trendlines bounding price action — one along the highs, one along the lows — forming a corridor for the trend.
Consolidation
A period of sideways, range-bound price movement with no clear directional trend. Represents a balance between buyers and sellers.
Crossover
When one moving average crosses above or below another — often used as a buy or sell trigger signal.
D
Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA)
Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price, reducing the impact of volatility on the average entry.
Down Bar
A candlestick whose closing price is lower than its opening price. Typically shown in red.
E
EMA (Exponential Moving Average)
A moving average that applies more weight to recent prices, making it faster and more responsive than a Simple Moving Average.
Entry / Exit Points
Specific price levels at which a trader initiates or closes a position.
F
False Breakout
Price moves briefly beyond a support or resistance level but quickly reverses back inside the range, trapping late participants.
Filled on Capitulation
Having a buy order execute during a panic-driven mass sell-off, often near a price bottom.
FOMO
Fear Of Missing Out. The emotional impulse to chase a rising price — a classic signal of market excess near a top.
H
Herd Mentality
The tendency for investors to follow crowd behaviour, often leading to exaggerated price swings in both directions.
Higher High / Higher Low
Price structures that define an uptrend: each rally peak and each pullback low exceeds the prior equivalent level.
Horizontal Consolidation
A range-bound period where price moves sideways within a relatively narrow horizontal zone.
L
Local Bottom / Local Top
A temporary low or high within a larger trend, offering short-term buying or selling opportunities without necessarily signalling a macro reversal.
Lower High / Lower Low
Price structures that define a downtrend: each bounce peak and each new trough is below the prior equivalent level.
M
Margin Call
A broker's demand that a trader deposit additional funds to cover losses and maintain the minimum required account balance.
Market Exuberance
Excessive optimism in the market leading to inflated prices beyond fundamental justification — a warning sign of an impending correction.
R
Rally
A sustained upward move in price, generally defined as a 10–20% increase from a recent low.
Resistance
A price level at which selling pressure is strong enough to prevent further upward movement. The "ceiling" of a range.
Rising Wedge
A bearish reversal pattern where price trends upward within converging trendlines — the upper boundary rising faster than the lower — before breaking down sharply.
S
Shakeout
A sharp, fast price move — usually a flush lower — that forces out weak or overleveraged holders before the real directional move begins.
SMA (Simple Moving Average)
The average closing price of an asset over a set period, giving equal weight to every session included.
Stink Bids
Extremely low limit buy orders placed well below the current market price, designed to fill only during panic-driven capitulation spikes.
Support
A price level at which buying interest is strong enough to prevent further decline. The "floor" of a range.
T
Trendline
A diagonal line drawn across successive highs or lows, used to identify trend direction, strength and potential reversal points.
Trading Range
The defined boundary between support and resistance within which price oscillates during consolidation.
V
Volatility
The degree to which an asset's price fluctuates over time. High volatility means large, rapid price swings — common in crypto markets.
